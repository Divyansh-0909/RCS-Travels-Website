# Offers lens
Score: 6/10 | Vote: FUND IF

This is an application of the Offers framework, not an endorsement by its author. The score reflects a plausible pain and a useful outcome with neither a defined paying segment nor demonstrated delivery. No customer purchases, measured time savings, or reliability results were supplied. All segment choices and test gates below are proposals.

## What is strong

The desired outcome is concrete: keep the character of a website the buyer already likes while making it work on other screen sizes. The buyer can compare the original, the adaptation, and the time needed to reach an acceptable result. This is easier to demonstrate than a broad promise to “make coding easier.” Working inside VS Code or the user's Codex workflow could reduce setup and handoff effort, provided the integration actually supports the intended experience.

My market assessment is mixed. **Pain: 7/10** for someone repeatedly fixing client sites under deadlines, but unmeasured for the broad proposed audience. **Spending power: 4/10** because “someone who made a website” includes hobbyists and businesses with very different budgets. **Reachability: 4/10** because no profession, stack, community, or acquisition channel is selected. **Growth: unscored** because the brief supplies no reliable evidence about this specific buying market. My starting hypothesis would be freelancers or small agencies delivering paid React/Tailwind marketing sites; this is a testable choice, not established demand.

For the value equation, where higher scores mean a stronger offer: **dream outcome 8/10; perceived likelihood 3/10; speed 6/10; low buyer effort 4/10.** The outcome is attractive. Likelihood is low because there is no portfolio of accepted adaptations. Speed is only potential until installation, preview setup, generation, revision, and agent implementation are included. Effort is the major weakness of workflow one: asking buyers to choose every breakpoint and manually repair layout could reproduce the job they hoped to avoid. Workflow two has the stronger commercial promise if AI produces a credible first adaptation and the visual editor makes exceptions easy.

A possible offer is: “Make your existing site ready for agreed mobile, tablet, and desktop widths, with a reviewable patch and preserved design intent.” Its useful stack would cover project connection, an original-design reference, proposed responsive layouts, visual correction, intermediate-width checks, and verified source changes. Prioritize elements that reduce review and rework; treat delivery cost as an open question for `/founder-cfo`.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)

1. **No buyer with recurring pain and a reason to pay.** An occasional user may tolerate manual fixes or ordinary agent prompts. Interest in a demonstration does not establish a business. The initial segment needs paid work, repeat use, and an accessible route to purchase.
2. **The promise fails at acceptance.** Attractive screenshots at three widths are insufficient if navigation, forms, content, or intermediate layouts break, or if the buyer dislikes the interpretation of the original. “Professional” needs explicit buyer acceptance criteria. In both workflows, an exported instruction file is only valuable when the resulting code preserves the approved edits.
3. **Correction and setup consume the savings.** Import trouble, selecting elements, managing conflicting overrides, and repeating agent handoffs can outweigh faster generation. Subscription pricing also fails if adaptation is infrequent. Name and price are undecided; neither should conceal these unknowns.

## The questions I would ask the founder

- Who has completed this job several times recently, what did each job cost in time or money, and who approves spending?
- Which layout decisions must preserve the original, and which may the AI reinterpret?
- What does the buyer receive: instructions, an applied patch, or an accepted site? How is failed handoff repaired?
- What would make the offer worth twice the eventual tested price: faster delivery, fewer revisions, stronger assurance, or something else?

## If FUND IF: the conditions

- Interview 12 buyers in one proposed segment about recent projects and recruit at least six who supply real sites for a pilot. Investigate contrary evidence rather than count compliments. Use `/founder-consumer` for simulated objections, clearly separate from these interviews.
- Test 12 adaptations, including both conversion directions, against each buyer's normal workflow. Measure total active time through accepted code. My proposed gate is at least 30% median time saved, buyer acceptance on at least nine tasks, and zero unresolved critical interaction regressions. These thresholds are judgment, not market facts.
- Obtain at least three paid pilots at a stated price, recording acquisition effort, revisions, and delivery cost. `/founder-pricing` should test the commercial model; `/founder-cfo` should establish whether support and compute leave a viable margin.
- Offer a bounded pilot assurance: refund the pilot fee if the agreed acceptance criteria cannot be met. Check affordability first. Use actual capacity limits or customer deadlines for urgency; avoid invented scarcity. Fund broader editor development only after the outcome and effort gates pass.
